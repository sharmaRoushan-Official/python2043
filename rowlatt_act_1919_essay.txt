ESSAY ON THE ROWLATT ACT OF 1919

Note: “Revolt Act of 1919” is not the standard historical name. In Indian history, this usually refers to the Rowlatt Act of 1919.

1. INTRODUCTION

The Rowlatt Act of 1919 was one of the most controversial laws introduced by the British government in India during the colonial period. It was passed to suppress revolutionary activities and political opposition against British rule. The Act gave the government extraordinary powers to arrest and detain people without trial in certain cases.

The Act was named after Justice Sidney Rowlatt, who chaired the committee responsible for investigating revolutionary activities in India. Indians regarded the legislation as a serious attack on civil liberties and fundamental rights. Mahatma Gandhi launched the Rowlatt Satyagraha against it, marking an important stage in the growth of India's national movement.

2. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

During the First World War (1914–1918), Indians supported the British government with soldiers, money and other resources. Many Indian leaders expected that their cooperation would lead to greater self-government after the war.

In 1917, the British government issued the Montagu Declaration, which promised the gradual development of responsible government in India. However, after the war, the British government continued to fear revolutionary movements and activities directed against colonial rule.

The British authorities appointed a committee under Justice Sidney Rowlatt in 1917 to investigate revolutionary conspiracies. Based on its recommendations, the government introduced legislation in 1919 to extend extraordinary wartime security powers into peacetime.

This legislation became known as the Rowlatt Act.

3. IMPORTANT FEATURES OF THE ROWLATT ACT, 1919

The Rowlatt Act was passed by the Imperial Legislative Council on 18 March 1919. Its formal name was the Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, 1919.

The major provisions were as follows:

1. Arrest without warrant: The government could arrest suspected individuals without the usual legal safeguards in specified circumstances.
2. Detention without trial: Suspected political activists could be detained without an ordinary criminal trial.
3. Special courts: Certain political cases could be tried by special tribunals under exceptional procedures.
4. Restrictions on appeals: Decisions of these special tribunals were not subject to the usual appeal process.
5. Restrictions on civil liberties: The law strengthened the government's ability to suppress revolutionary activities and political opposition.

These provisions created widespread fear that the British government could misuse its powers against innocent people and nationalists.

4. WHY WAS THE ROWLATT ACT CALLED THE BLACK ACT?

The Rowlatt Act was popularly known as the Black Act because it threatened individual liberty and the principles of justice.

The British government had introduced emergency measures during the First World War to control revolutionary activities. Indians expected those extraordinary powers to end after the war. Instead, the Rowlatt Act continued similar repressive measures during peacetime.

The law was particularly unpopular because it allowed severe restrictions on personal liberty without the ordinary protections of criminal justice. Indian members of the Imperial Legislative Council strongly opposed the legislation.

Consequently, the Act became a symbol of British repression and the denial of political rights in colonial India.

5. MAHATMA GANDHI AND THE ROWLATT SATYAGRAHA

Mahatma Gandhi strongly opposed the Rowlatt Act because he believed that an unjust law should be resisted through peaceful means.

In February 1919, Gandhi began organising resistance to the proposed legislation. He established the Satyagraha Sabha to mobilise people against the Act.

The main methods of protest included:
- Peaceful demonstrations and public meetings.
- Hartals, involving the closure of shops and suspension of ordinary activities.
- Fasting and prayer.
- Civil disobedience and a commitment to non-violence.

Gandhi called for a nationwide hartal on 6 April 1919. People from different communities and social backgrounds participated in demonstrations across India.

The movement marked an important transition from limited political agitation to mass participation in the freedom struggle.

6. JALLIANWALA BAGH MASSACRE, 1919

The Rowlatt Act intensified political tension in Punjab, particularly in Amritsar.

Two prominent nationalist leaders, Dr. Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr. Satyapal, were arrested on 10 April 1919. Their arrest provoked protests among local residents.

On 13 April 1919, thousands of people gathered at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar. The gathering included people who had assembled to protest and others who were present for different reasons, including the Baisakhi festival.

Brigadier-General Reginald Edward Harry Dyer ordered his troops to open fire on the crowd. Hundreds of people were killed, and many more were injured. The precise death toll remains disputed.

The massacre shocked India and exposed the brutality of British colonial rule. Rabindranath Tagore renounced his knighthood in protest, while nationalist opposition to British rule grew stronger.

Although the massacre was not itself a provision of the Rowlatt Act, it occurred during the same period of political repression and became closely associated with the anti-Rowlatt movement.

7. IMPACT OF THE ROWLATT ACT ON THE INDIAN FREEDOM MOVEMENT

The Rowlatt Act had several important consequences.

First, it strengthened nationalism. People who had hoped for political reforms after the First World War became increasingly dissatisfied with British rule.

Second, it brought Gandhi to the forefront of national politics. The Rowlatt Satyagraha helped establish him as a leader capable of mobilising people across India.

Third, it exposed the limitations of British reforms. The government spoke of gradual self-government while simultaneously introducing repressive legislation.

Fourth, it encouraged mass political participation. Workers, traders, students and other sections of society joined protests in different regions.

Fifth, it contributed to the growth of the Non-Cooperation Movement. The Rowlatt Act, the Jallianwala Bagh massacre and the Khilafat issue became important factors behind the nationwide movement launched in 1920.

Finally, it weakened Indian faith in British justice. The events of 1919 convinced many nationalists that meaningful freedom required a much stronger struggle against colonial rule.

8. WITHDRAWAL AND REPEAL

The Rowlatt Act faced sustained opposition from Indian nationalists and the wider public. The agitation against it severely damaged the credibility of the British government's claims to govern fairly.

The Act was subsequently repealed in 1922, under the Special Laws Repeal Act, 1922.

Its legacy remained significant because the campaign against it demonstrated the power of organised, non-violent mass resistance.

9. CONCLUSION

The Rowlatt Act of 1919 was a major turning point in the history of India's struggle for independence. It represented the continuation of colonial repression at a time when Indians expected greater political freedom after the First World War.

The Act provoked widespread protests under Mahatma Gandhi's leadership and contributed to the political atmosphere in which the Jallianwala Bagh massacre occurred. These events weakened Indian confidence in British justice and strengthened the demand for self-government.

Ultimately, the Rowlatt Satyagraha helped lay the foundation for the mass movements that followed, especially the Non-Cooperation Movement of 1920–1922. It remains an important example of the conflict between colonial authority and civil liberties.

EXAM CLARIFICATION

Do not confuse the Rowlatt Act, 1919, with the Government of India Act, 1919. The Rowlatt Act was a repressive security law; the Government of India Act, 1919, introduced constitutional reforms, including dyarchy in the provinces.
